Fraud Prevention & Investigation Guide #03

How to Find a Cryptocurrency Transaction Hash

Published October 2026 · Educational information

Where transaction IDs and hashes appear on common wallets and exchanges, and why investigators need them. This article is educational information, not legal, financial, or law-enforcement advice.

Why this matters

Cryptocurrency fraud often combines urgency, convincing stories, and irreversible payment methods. The first priority is to slow down, preserve information, and separate facts from assumptions. A clear record can help legitimate platforms, investigators, attorneys, or authorities understand what happened.

Key warning signs

Watch for pressure to act immediately, promises of guaranteed or unusually high returns, requests to pay new fees before withdrawals, unfamiliar websites, requests for passwords or recovery phrases, and people who discourage independent verification. No single warning sign proves fraud, but several together deserve serious caution.

What to document

Save transaction hashes, wallet addresses, dates and times, screenshots, emails, chat messages, payment receipts, website addresses, usernames, phone numbers, and the names used by the people involved. Keep original files when possible and avoid editing the only copy.

What not to do

Do not send additional cryptocurrency simply because someone promises to unlock funds or recover a previous loss. Do not share seed phrases, private keys, passwords, one-time authentication codes, or remote-access credentials. Do not delete messages that may later help establish the timeline.

Next steps

Create a short chronological timeline, identify every transaction involved, and report the incident to the relevant legitimate platform or authority. If funds moved through an exchange, ask that exchange about its fraud-reporting process. Recovery outcomes vary and should never be guaranteed.
